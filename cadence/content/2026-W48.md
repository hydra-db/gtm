# LinkedIn drafts, 2026-W48

## 1. Proof point: Benchmarks that lead with questions

**Hook:** We deleted our best benchmark line from our homepage. Someone beat it.

We deleted our best benchmark line from our homepage. Someone beat it.

That's how leaderboards work in agent retrieval and memory. A lead is rented, not owned. Somebody passes you in a few weeks and your headline turns into a footnote.

So we rebuilt our benchmark page around the question instead of the score.

Company brain: questions answered across 511,958 internal documents, many of them duplicated, outdated or contradictory. That's what a real company drive looks like. Score: 88.7.

Code: "how does this library parse a string?", where the answer only exists across many files. Score: 90.73.

Memory: what changed, and in what order, over a million tokens of history. Temporal reasoning: 91.

Finance: "Why did margins recover in Q3?", traced back to the original filings. 91.4% Recall@10.

The question tells you whether a benchmark looks like your workload. The score only tells you how we did on it.

Two rules I hold us to now:

1. A range beats one number. Speed varies by query type and hop depth, so one number either overclaims or underclaims depending on which slice you picked.

2. Publish the method. We're putting datasets and methodology on GitHub so anyone can replicate the results. If you can't rerun it, it's marketing.

Next time a vendor (us included) shows you one big number, ask which question it answered and how it was measured.

**First comment:** github.com/hydra-db/benchmark: our public graph DB benchmark repo, method included.

## 2. Objection: We are not a memory company

**Hook:** We're not a memory company. This week I rejected a draft that called us one.

We're not a memory company. This week I rejected a draft that called us one.

It's an easy mistake. Memory for agents became a crowded shelf this year: five or more look-alike products, compared against each other in every roundup.

But memory is the job, not the product. When someone asks "so you're an alternative to X?", the honest answer is no. We're what you build your memory on.

Here's the difference in practice. A hosted memory layer gives one agent remember and recall. For a single-agent prototype that's faster, and I'd tell you to use it.

It stops fitting when your agents have to answer three questions:

What has to be shared across agents?
What has to be isolated per user and per org?
What has to update when a fact changes?

That's not a memory feature. That's your context: your entities, the relationships between them, the history of your decisions. You want to own that. Talk to 100 developers and my guess is about 90 will tell you they want to own their memory.

So we ship primitives: graph storage on object storage, ontology building, agent traces. You assemble the context store your agents need, and if that's a memory layer, you build your own on top of us.

LangChain didn't grow by saying "we're a hosted RAG solution". It grew by teaching people how to do RAG. That's our bet too: teach the pattern, let the tool show up in it.

If you're choosing a memory product right now, write down your answers to those three questions first.

## 3. Learned this week: Our graph was read late

**Hook:** A customer turned our knowledge graph on and off. The results were identical. They were right.

A customer turned our knowledge graph on and off. The results were identical. They were right.

They're an AI startup. Their question: when a clause in one document affects other documents, find every document it touches.

They ran a careful A/B. Fresh databases, same queries, each run twice. Graph on, graph off: identical ranks. The graph only described documents the search had already returned.

Then they rephrased the query as what the clause reaches. That found every expected document. A query rewrite beat our graph.

We reproduced it. The cause was simple and fully ours: fast mode read graph context after ranking. By the time the graph spoke, the top results were already picked.

And similarity ranking fails in a specific way here. A document that repeats a clause's language can score above the document the clause actually governs. Sounding alike is not the same as "this clause applies to that".

So the fix is about where the graph enters, not whether you have one: pull each clause's references from the original files, with their direction and the passage that states them, and use them before ranking. It's in progress, not shipped.

My note to the team: here is a real use case and we don't do this well. Drop the random experiments and fix real stuff.

A customer who shares their scripts and raw results is the best benchmark you will ever get.

If you added a graph to your retrieval stack, check where it enters the pipeline before you decide whether it works.
